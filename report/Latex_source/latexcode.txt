\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
\noindent This report investigates the design and deployment of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for extreme edge devices, focusing on the automated classification of agricultural rice grains. We systematically develop a standard CNN baseline (Model A) and a highly optimized Depthwise-Separable CNN (Model B) strictly constrained to under 100,000 parameters. Through rigorous empirical evaluation, we demonstrate that Model B achieves a 94\% reduction in memory footprint ($\sim$0.99 MB) compared to State-of-the-Art architectures like EfficientNet-B0, whilst maintaining a highly competitive test accuracy of 96.73\%. This research validates the viability of deploying highly accurate, custom-built parameter-efficient networks on low-resource microcontrollers.

\section{Dataset \& Pre-processing Pipeline}
\vspace{1.5em}
\subsection{Dataset Motivation \& Description}
The dataset utilized is the Kaggle Rice Image Dataset. The objective is to classify five distinct macro geometric varieties of rice grains: \textbf{Arborio, Basmati, Ipsala, Jasmine, and Karacadag}. This simulates a real-world edge deployment scenario, such as an automated agricultural sorting machine operating on a resource-constrained microcontroller.

The classification task is particularly suitable for evaluating lightweight convolutional neural networks because the rice varieties exhibit differences in their geometric and visual characteristics. Features such as grain shape, aspect ratio, size, texture, and surface appearance can provide useful spatial information for CNN-based feature extraction.

Since the primary objective of this assignment is to investigate CNN architectures under strict computational and memory constraints, the dataset was intentionally processed using a fixed input resolution and a controlled number of samples per class. This allows the effect of architectural modifications on model size, computational complexity, and classification performance to be evaluated under consistent experimental conditions.


\vspace{1.5em}
\subsection{Data Pre-processing Pipeline \& Splitting}
To ensure robust model training and emulate low-resource hardware constraints, the raw data underwent a strict pre-processing pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Step \arabic*:}, leftmargin=2.5cm, labelwidth=1.8cm, labelsep=0.5em]
    \item \textbf{Image Acquisition:} The original dataset consists of RGB images containing individual rice grains. The raw images may have different spatial resolutions; therefore, a common input representation was required before they could be supplied to the CNN architectures.
    \item \textbf{Resolution Downscaling:} Images strictly resized to $64 \times 64 \times 3$ tensors to minimize memory overhead. The first two dimensions represent the spatial resolution, while the third dimension represents the three RGB colour channels. Reducing the image resolution significantly decreases the amount of image data processed by each convolutional layer and therefore helps reduce the computational and memory requirements of the neural network.
    \newpage
    \item \textbf{Sub-sampling:} Exactly 2,000 images were selected from each rice variety, producing a total dataset of 10,000 images. Equal sampling was adopted to prevent the training process from being biased toward a particular rice variety due to unequal class representation.
    \item \textbf{Pixel Normalization:} Rescaling pixel intensities from $[0, 255]$ to $[0, 1]$ or $[-1, 1]$ depending on the network architecture to ensure gradient stability.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{1.5em}

\noindent A rigid 70/15/15 stratified split was enforced to ensure equal class distributions across all sets:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=4em]
    \item \textbf{70\% Training data:} 7,000 images utilized for gradient descent.
    \item \textbf{15\% Validation data:} 1,500 images utilized for hyperparameter tuning.
    \item \textbf{15\% Test data:} 1,500 images utilized for unbiased final evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Framework \& Optimizer Dynamics}
\vspace{0.5cm}
\subsection{Activation Functions: Overcoming the Vanishing Gradient}
Standard non-linear activation functions such as Sigmoid $\sigma(x)$ or Hyperbolic Tangent $\tanh(x)$ frequently encounter the \textit{Vanishing Gradient} problem. During backpropagation, their derivatives approach zero at the tails, which prevents weights in earlier layers from updating effectively.

\noindent To counteract this, we exclusively integrated the \textbf{Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU)}:
\begin{equation}
    f(x) = \max(0, x)
\end{equation}
ReLU provides a constant gradient of 1 for all positive inputs, allowing deep networks to converge rapidly. Furthermore, its simplistic threshold mechanism avoids computationally expensive exponential operations, which is crucial for edge microcontrollers.

\subsection{Optimizer Dynamics: SGD vs. Momentum vs. Adam}
Selecting the correct optimization algorithm dictates the trajectory of weight updates. We evaluated multiple algorithms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard SGD:} Updates weights based solely on the current batch's gradient. It frequently oscillates in ravines and gets trapped in saddle points.
    \item \textbf{SGD with Momentum:} Introduces a velocity term ($\gamma$) that accumulates historical gradients:
    \begin{equation}
        v_t = \gamma v_{t-1} + \eta \nabla_\theta J(\theta)
    \end{equation}
    This damps vertical oscillations and accelerates horizontal convergence.
    \item \textbf{Adam (Adaptive Moment Estimation):} The optimizer selected for our final training pipeline. Adam merges the velocity benefits of Momentum with the adaptive per-parameter learning rates of RMSprop. As visualized in Figure \ref{fig:optimizers}, Adam provided significantly faster and more stable initial reductions in validation loss compared to SGD.
\end{itemize}

\section{Standard CNN Architecture (Model A)}
\vspace{0.5cm}
\subsection{Architectural Pipeline \& Design Philosophy}
Model A acts as our high-capacity, standard CNN baseline. The architectural pipeline consists of two primary blocks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Feature Extraction Block:} Interleaved 2D Convolutions and $2 \times 2$ Max Pooling layers designed to extract spatial hierarchies (edges, textures, shapes).
    \item \textbf{Classification Head:} A flattened tensor fed into deeply connected Dense layers terminating in a 5-node Softmax probability output.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Mathematical Parameter Formulation}
The trainable parameters for a standard Conv2D layer are calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    Parameters = (K_h \times K_w \times C_{in} + 1) \times C_{out}
\end{equation}

\begin{align*}
    \text{Conv2D\_1: } & (3 \times 3 \times 3 + 1) \times 32 &&= 896 \\
    \text{Conv2D\_2: } & (3 \times 3 \times 32 + 1) \times 64 &&= 18,496 \\
    \text{Conv2D\_3: } & (3 \times 3 \times 64 + 1) \times 128 &&= 73,856 \\
    \text{Dense\_1: } & (8192 + 1) \times 128 &&= 1,048,704 \\
    \text{Dense\_2: } & (128 + 1) \times 5 &&= 645
\end{align*}

\section{Resource-Constrained Edge CNN (Model B)}
\vspace{0.5cm}
\subsection{The Edge Computing Paradigm}
Edge devices possess highly restrictive memory budgets. The assignment enforces a strict limit of $<100,000$ parameters. To achieve this without losing spatial resolution, Model B replaces standard convolutions with \textbf{Depthwise-Separable Convolutions}.

\subsection{Depthwise Separable Mechanics}
This technique factorizes a standard convolution into two distinct mathematical phases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Depthwise Phase:} Applies a single spatial filter ($3 \times 3$) independently to each input channel.
    \item \textbf{Pointwise Phase:} Applies a $1 \times 1$ convolution across all channels to linearly combine the depthwise outputs.
\end{enumerate}
This factorization drastically reduces computational Multiply-Accumulate (MAC) operations. The new parameter formula becomes:
\begin{equation}
    Parameters = (K_h \times K_w \times C_{in}) + (1 \times 1 \times C_{in} \times C_{out} + C_{out})
\end{equation}
\newpage
\subsection{Optimized Architectural Pipeline}
Model B further reduces parameters by replacing the massive Flatten layer with Global Average Pooling (GAP), which averages the spatial dimensions into a single vector.

\section{State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Transfer Learning}

\subsection{Transfer Learning Pipeline}
To contextualize the performance of our custom network, we utilized transfer learning on two pre-trained architectures designed for mobile computing. The pipeline involved:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Base Layer Freezing:} Freezing the ImageNet-trained convolutional base to preserve generalized feature extraction and prevent catastrophic forgetting.
    \item \textbf{Input Scaling:} Adapting raw $[0, 255]$ pixels to $[-1, 1]$ (for MobileNetV2) using dedicated Rescaling layers.
    \item \textbf{Custom Head Attachment:} Attaching a Global Average Pooling layer followed by custom Dense layers for 5-class identification.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{SOTA Architectures Selected}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MobileNetV2:} Utilizes Inverted Residuals and Linear Bottlenecks to drastically reduce the dimensionality of layers, optimizing it for mobile GPUs.
    \item \textbf{EfficientNetB0:} Utilizes Compound Scaling, which balances network depth, width, and resolution using a mathematically optimized scalar coefficient.
\end{itemize}

\section{Advantages \& Limitations in Edge Deployment}

Building a custom sub-100k parameter model from scratch (Model B) presents a unique set of strategic trade-offs when compared against fine-tuning industry-standard SOTA models.

\subsection{Advantages of Custom Lightweight Models (Model B)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Extreme Memory Efficiency:} Occupying less than 1 MB, Model B can be easily flashed onto basic Edge IoT devices (e.g., ESP32, STM32) which lack the storage to hold an 18 MB EfficientNet model.
    \item \textbf{Lower Inference Latency:} The massive reduction in total parameters directly correlates to fewer floating-point operations per second (FLOPS) required during forward propagation, resulting in faster real-time sorting.
    \item \textbf{Inherent Regularization:} Due to its heavily constrained capacity, Model B inherently resists overfitting without requiring complex Dropout layers or L1/L2 regularization tuning.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations of Custom Lightweight Models}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Longer Training Convergence:} Pre-trained SOTA models already possess highly optimized feature-extracting kernels from ImageNet. Training Model B from scratch requires the optimizer to learn fundamental geometrical kernels from zero, increasing the required epochs for convergence.
    \item \textbf{Sub-optimal Maximum Accuracy:} SOTA architectures leverage complex innovations (Inverted Residuals) to extract deeply nuanced features. For highly complex datasets, a simple custom Depthwise CNN will inevitably hit an accuracy ceiling lower than an EfficientNet architecture.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion \& Future Work}
This project rigorously investigated Convolutional Neural Networks for resource-constrained edge classification. Model A provided strong accuracy but overfit the training data and consumed excessive memory (13.44 MB). SOTA models achieved the absolute highest accuracies ($\sim 99\%$) but remain unsuitable for ultra-low-resource hardware due to their massive parameter counts ($\sim 4.1\text{M}$). 

Our custom \textbf{Model B (Lightweight CNN)} successfully satisfied the strict requirement of $< 100,000$ parameters, achieving an astonishingly small memory footprint of just \textbf{0.99 MB} while maintaining a highly competitive \textbf{96.73\%} test accuracy. For environments with rigorous physical hardware constraints, Model B demonstrates the optimal strategic trade-off. Future work could involve applying Post-Training Quantization (INT8) to further reduce Model B's size to under 250 KB for extreme micro-edge deployment.
